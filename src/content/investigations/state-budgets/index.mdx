Most of a state budget is inertia. Medicaid, schools and payroll arrive every
year whether anyone decides anything or not, and the line on the chart slopes
gently upward. The interesting part is what happens when something interrupts
that — and whether the interruption shows up in the numbers at all.

This is less an argument than an instrument. Every state has a page showing
what it spent, what it borrowed, who was governor, and every year-over-year
movement large enough to be worth asking about. Some of those movements have
obvious causes. Some don't. A few turn out not to be real at all.

## Three things that look identical on a chart

The hardest part of this was not finding jumps in the data. It was working out
which ones mean anything.

A **real event** looks like Florida in FY2023, when spending on what Census
files under commercial activities went from $1.2 billion to $14.1 billion and
back down again. That category, per the Census classification manual, covers
state disaster insurance — and Hurricane Ian had made landfall that September,
with $2.9 billion in federal public assistance obligated behind it. The state
was backstopping a collapsing property insurance market.

A **policy change** looks like Nevada in FY2023, when K-12 spending rose 99% and
stayed there. No disaster, no emergency — a legislature decided to spend more on
schools, and the new level persisted. That is a choice, not a shock, and the
pages label it differently.

An **artifact** looks like Massachusetts in FY2014, when K-12 spending appeared
to collapse from $5.4 billion to $1.0 billion while administrative spending
tripled in the same year — and both snapped back the next. Nothing happened.
Money was booked to the wrong line. Every state page lists these separately,
under the movements excluded as data artifacts, because presenting a
bookkeeping error as a policy decision is the fastest way to be confidently
wrong.

<Callout>
  Where a federally declared disaster falls in the same fiscal year as a
  movement, it is shown alongside it. That is a coincidence in time, not proof
  of cause — and disasters are only shown against spending, borrowing and
  federal aid, because a hurricane does not raise income tax receipts.
</Callout>

## What the governor bands do and don't tell you

Each state's timeline is shaded by who held the governorship. It is the obvious
thing to want to look at, and the easiest thing to over-read.

Governors preside over budgets more than they control them. Medicaid enrolment,
federal aid formulas and school funding statutes drive most of the year-to-year
movement, and they do not care who won the last election. Two of the largest
movements in this dataset — Florida's insurance intervention and the flood of
federal pandemic money in FY2020 and FY2021 — would have happened under anyone.

The bands are there so you can see the correlation and judge it, not so the
site can claim a cause.

## Sources & methods

The spending, revenue and debt figures come from the **Census Bureau's Annual
Survey of State Government Finances**, which puts every state on a common
classification rather than each state's own budget categories. Totals here are
computed from the detail item codes rather than taken from Census's published
aggregates; for every year where the two definitions agree, they match exactly,
for all fifty states.

Disasters come from **FEMA's OpenFEMA declarations**, with federal obligations
attached, mapped into each state's own fiscal year — which is why Hurricane Ian,
in September 2022, counts as FY2023 in Florida but FY2022 in Alabama. Governor
terms come from **Wikidata**, hand-corrected where a party switch made the
record ambiguous. Population is Census; inflation adjustment uses the BEA GDP
price deflator.

Four limits worth stating plainly:

- **The range is FY2012 to FY2024.** Census's API advertises data back to 1967
  but returns nothing before 2012. That means the 2001 and 2008–09 recessions
  are out of reach, and only the pandemic is visible here.
- **Census changed its own definitions between FY2021 and FY2022**, and dropped a
  lot of item-code detail at the same time. Everything here is computed on one
  definition across all years to avoid a false step in the middle of every
  chart, but categories that shifted are flagged on each state page.
- **There is no Medicaid line.** Census folds it into public welfare, so that
  category is mostly, but not only, Medicaid.
- **Debt owed steps down in FY2022 without anything being repaid.** Through
  FY2021 the published total includes conduit debt — bonds a state issues for
  private borrowers such as industrial developers and hospitals, who repay them.
  It counts against the state on paper without burdening its taxpayers, and it
  is a third of reported state debt nationally. Accounting standards changed and
  Census stopped reporting it from FY2022, so the total falls without anything
  being paid off.
